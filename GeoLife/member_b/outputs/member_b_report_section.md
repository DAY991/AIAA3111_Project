# Member B: Feature Changes, Model Dependence, and Deployment Context

## 1. Methods and comparability

We analysed the frozen GeoLife deployment experiment (protocol v1.0.0; A run `04d2f3dee1b64ac6e6d359b0aae75128a001a8cd25ac2f4df9afce4a389a1d50`). The reference consists of 90,120 clean usable 120-second windows from 54 users. The primary paired analysis uses the 71,941 windows from 48 users that remain usable under every main sampling condition. All four representative conditions—`ds0_n0`, `ds10_n0`, `ds0_n20`, and `ds10_n20`—therefore use the same persistent window IDs and labels. `ds0` retains native irregular sampling; `n0` adds no synthetic error to already observed GPS coordinates. `ds10` retains the first point in each 10-second time bucket; `n20` adds independent Gaussian noise with a 20 m standard deviation on each local coordinate axis. The same shared degradation implementation and primary noise seed 0 were used throughout.

For feature drift, we recalculated all 17 Core A statistical features for each condition and subtracted the corresponding clean-window feature value. We report original-unit medians of the signed paired differences and medians/interquartile ranges of their absolute values. The six fixed groups are speed, acceleration, heading change, distance, sampling/time, and stop ratio. Group summaries retain feature-level values rather than summing different physical units. Class- and user-level diagnostic rows are available separately. Nonfinite feature values followed A's prediction rule (replacement by zero).

For model dependence, each common-support user was predicted only by the RF trained without that user. The 48 fold-specific models and their hashes were verified against A's handoff. Before permutation, our predictions matched A's saved predictions for every analysed fold and condition. Within each held-out user, all columns in one feature group were jointly permuted using the same row order, while other columns remained fixed. The same user/group/repeat permutation was reused across the four conditions. We used 10 repeats with base seed 20261112 and one RF thread. For each repeat, predictions were pooled across all 48 users before calculating fixed five-class Macro-F1. Group importance is original pooled F1 minus permuted pooled F1; negative values remain in the results. The reported permutation standard deviation measures the 10 random permutations of fixed data and models.

## 2. Paired feature drift

Time thinning changes both sampling descriptors and motion estimates. Relative to native sampling, `ds10_n0` has a median of 44 fewer points per window and an 8-second larger mean time gap. Its median paired mean-speed change is approximately -0.24 m/s, and its median absolute mean-speed change is approximately 0.29 m/s.

Added coordinate noise has a much stronger effect on the dense-sampling speed estimate. In `ds0_n20`, point count and mean gap are unchanged, while the median paired mean-speed change is approximately +12.10 m/s. In `ds10_n20`, the corresponding median signed change is approximately +1.32 m/s and the median absolute change approximately 1.42 m/s. These estimates come from the same 71,941 windows. The smaller speed disturbance after thinning is consistent with the fact that displacement noise is divided by a longer time gap, while thinning simultaneously removes information about the actual motion. Detailed values for all features are in `feature_drift_by_feature.csv`; the class/user breakdown is in `feature_drift_by_class_user.csv`.

## 3. Dependence of the fixed RF on feature groups

The clean RF has pooled Macro-F1 0.6344, identical to A's primary common-support grid. Its speed-group permutation importance is 0.3572 F1 points, much larger than acceleration (0.0479), heading change (0.0463), distance (0.0204), sampling/time (0.0108), and stop ratio (0.0130). Under this test, the clean RF depends strongly on the speed summary columns.

At `ds10_n0`, RF Macro-F1 is 0.3551 and speed importance decreases to 0.1844. Acceleration importance is -0.0268: on average, permuting these columns increases the score of the already degraded RF. At `ds0_n20`, RF Macro-F1 is 0.1096 and speed importance is 0.0055; most groups are near zero. This pattern is compatible with a model near the score floor and does not imply that the underlying features carry no information. At `ds10_n20`, RF Macro-F1 is 0.2394 and speed importance is 0.0720, greater than under `ds0_n20`. The complete 10-repeat values and standard deviations are in `group_permutation_importance.csv` and `group_permutation_summary.csv`.

## 4. Comparison with classification performance

A's 12-cell common-support grid shows GRU above RF in pooled Macro-F1 at every principal point estimate, but the gap is nonmonotone. The largest seed-0 gap is 14.28 percentage points at `ds5_n10`; the clean gap is 2.61 points. This absolute gap must be distinguished from an increase over the clean gap. For example, the `ds10_n10` seed-0 gap is 2.66 points, only about 0.05 points above clean.

The RF score itself is also nonmonotone: it rises from 0.3551 at `ds10_n0` to 0.4190 at `ds10_n5`, and from 0.2129 at `ds0_n10` to 0.4028 at `ds10_n10`. In the four representative conditions, `ds10_n20` RF Macro-F1 (0.2394) exceeds `ds0_n20` (0.1096). The paired drift shows that time thinning greatly reduces the *synthetic-noise-induced* inflation of mean speed; grouped permutation shows more residual speed dependence in `ds10_n20` than in `ds0_n20`. Taken together, these observations support a plausible difference-noise mechanism for part of the score pattern. They do not isolate that mechanism causally or show that less frequent real-world collection is generally better. Thinning also loses movement detail, and the fixed RF interacts with correlated input groups.

For descriptive two-factor comparisons, `member_b_interaction_from_grid.csv` uses `I = F(ds,n) - F(ds,0) - F(0,n) + F(0,0)` on the identical common support. Positive `I` means the combined F1 loss is smaller than the sum of the separate losses *on the bounded F1 scale*; it is not evidence of physical cancellation. When an additive reference falls below zero, the F1 floor especially constrains interpretation. No significance claim is made from the single-seed cells.

## 5. Scope, literature, and deployment limits

The common support is 79.83% of the 90,120 clean usable reference windows; six reference users have no common windows. The paired grid describes these surviving windows, while class and user coverage must be presented separately. At 30-second thinning, no windows meet the frozen minimum-point and maximum-gap rule; classification F1 is undefined, not zero. Absolute GRU advantage does not guarantee adequate performance for every mode: A's `ds10_n20` class results, for instance, show RF above GRU for walk and bus despite GRU's higher overall Macro-F1.

The 12 conditions in `scenario_mapping.csv` are illustrative quality stresses, not calibrated phone power modes or identified street environments. The added Gaussian noise does not model urban GNSS bias, correlated multipath/NLOS errors, heavy tails, or missing observations. Burkhard et al. (2020; DOI 10.1016/j.trc.2020.01.021) already evaluated transport-mode detection under deteriorating spatial and temporal quality, so combining those two axes alone is not a novelty claim. Wang et al. (2024; DOI 10.3969/j.issn.0258-2724.20220136) studied actual cellular signalling at much coarser sampling scales; its recommended frequency does not transfer to these 120-second GPS windows. Weng et al. (2023; DOI 10.1016/j.measurement.2023.113766) motivates concern about urban smartphone GNSS NLOS and multipath, but its reported positioning accuracy does not calibrate our per-axis Gaussian sigma. The checked evidence and transfer limits are recorded in `literature_evidence.csv`.

Permutation importance describes dependence of these *fixed* RF models on their inputs under each test distribution. Correlated groups, the F1 floor, and the selected common support restrict interpretation. Its repeat standard deviations are neither cross-user confidence intervals nor training-stability estimates. Both evaluated pipelines use windows formed from known same-label segments; a live device would also have to detect mode transitions without those labels. Thus the study supports relative performance and failure-boundary discussion under a reproducible offline protocol, not a claim that either model is ready for deployment.
